Insights

Notes from the work.

Short, practical notes on building Mobile Integrated Health programs that last — written from the work, not the theory. Each one answers a question we hear from chiefs, EMS directors, and health system leaders.

Measurement

The baseline window closes the day you enroll your first patient.

Most MIH programs get approved on a demand argument — too many 911 calls from the same addresses, too many ED visits from patients who don’t need an emergency department. Then they get measured on activity: how many visits, how many enrollments, how many referrals. The problem is that the numbers that would prove the program worked were never captured, because nobody captured them before the first patient enrolled.

A before-and-after requires a before. Once a program starts changing outcomes, the pre-intervention picture is gone. Capture the baseline before you start: 911 calls and ED visits for each enrolled patient, 90 days before first contact and 90 days after, plus what one avoided transport actually costs your agency. The window closes the day you enroll your first patient.

Measurement

Four numbers to capture before you start.

Programs rarely end because the clinical work stopped being good. They end in a budget meeting, when someone asks what the program returned and the honest answer is a set of stories rather than a number. The reason is almost always the same: nobody captured the baseline. These four measures take very little effort to set up at the outset and cannot be reconstructed later.

01

911 calls, 90 days before

For each enrolled patient, the number of 911 responses in the 90 days prior to first program contact. Source: your CAD / ePCR, by patient identifier or address.

02

ED visits, 90 days before

Emergency department visits for the same patient over the same 90 days. Source: hospital partner, HIE, or payer data. Requires an agreement — start this early, it is the slowest piece.

03

911 calls and ED visits, 90 days after

The same two measures for the 90 days following first contact. Set the pull date at enrollment so it runs automatically.

04

Local cost per avoided transport

What one avoided ambulance response and transport actually costs your agency. Source: your own budget — total EMS cost divided by responses, or a unit-hour cost model.

Setting it up

Fix the enrollment date for every patient — all before-and-after windows run from that single date. Decide your patient identifier now; changing it midstream breaks the dataset. Start the ED data conversation early. Write the definitions down — what counts as a contact, an enrollment, an avoided transport — in a document that outlives whoever is running the program. And automate the pull if you can: a baseline that depends on someone remembering to run a report will not survive year two.

Three mistakes that cost programs their funding

Reporting activity instead of outcomes. Visits made, referrals received, and patients enrolled all show the program is busy. None of them show it worked. No comparison. Even a rough one helps: the same patients before versus after, or enrolled patients versus similar patients who were not reached. Changing definitions midstream. A redefined measure resets your trend line to zero. Lock the definitions and live with imperfect ones rather than better ones applied inconsistently.

Twelve to eighteen months from now, when continuation funding, a levy, or a budget cycle puts the program under scrutiny, you will be able to say: for the patients this program engaged, 911 use fell by this much, emergency department visits fell by this much, and the avoided responses were worth this much. That is a different conversation than describing good work.

Demand

Finding your addressable volume.

Not every 911 call is an MIH call. Addressable volume — the share of total call volume a demand-side approach could realistically affect — typically runs 15–30% of total call volume: the frequent callers, the avoidable transports, and the gaps in primary and behavioral health access driving the volume.

Report it as a range, and build the case on the conservative end. A program sized on the optimistic end is a program that disappoints its funders in year one.
